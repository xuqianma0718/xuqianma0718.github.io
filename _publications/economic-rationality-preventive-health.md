abstract: >-
  Research on preventive health behavior uses cognitive ability — measured in health surveys by numeracy and related tests — as a proxy for decision-making ability. Using the Understanding America Study, I compare quantitative ability (a composite of numeracy and financial-literacy tests) with choice consistency, a revealed-preference measure of how well incentivized choices under risk fit a stable preference ordering, as predictors of influenza vaccination from 2014 to 2026 and COVID-19 vaccination during the 2021 rollout. The two diverge systematically. For influenza, choice consistency becomes more positively associated with uptake after 2020, while the change in quantitative ability is similar in magnitude but statistically insignificant. During the COVID-19 rollout the pattern reverses: quantitative ability predicts uptake in later phases, while the choice-consistency association weakens during eligibility expansion. The measures are not substitutes: quantitative ability matters where information is new, choice consistency where benefits and costs must be weighed. This is the first application of revealed-preference consistency measures to preventive health behavior.
