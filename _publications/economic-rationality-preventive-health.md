abstract: >-
  Vaccination is a choice under uncertainty that draws on two different decision making abilities: forming beliefs about risks and making fewer mistakes when making the same kind of choice again and again. Using the Understanding America Study, I measure the first with cognitive tests of quantitative ability and the second with choice consistency in an incentivized revealed-preference experiment. For influenza (2014–2026), neither predicts vaccination on average, but receipt falls over time especially post COVID among the least consistent adults, most sharply among Republicans, and the consistency gap grows most among adults with fewer chronic conditions, groups likely to perceive less risk. For COVID-19 (2021), quantitative ability predicts who got vaccinated earlier. Higher-ability adults held more favorable beliefs about vaccine safety and effectiveness before most could get a dose, and these beliefs account for about 60 percent of the gap. After adjusting for baseline beliefs, lower-ability adults more often reported not knowing how to book or where to go. This points to prosocial and norm-based messages from messengers people trust for familiar vaccines, and to early, clear safety information and appointment guidance for new ones.
