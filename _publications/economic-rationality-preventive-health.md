abstract: >-
  Uptake of familiar and new vaccines is associated with different decision-making abilities. Using the Understanding America Study, I compare test-based quantitative ability with an incentivized, revealed-preference measure of choice consistency as predictors of influenza vaccination (2014–2026) and COVID-19 vaccination (2021). For influenza, the vaccination gap between less and more consistent respondents widens over the sample period, driven by falling receipt among the least consistent, while receipt falls by similar amounts across quantitative-ability groups. The widening is concentrated among Republicans and respondents with fewer chronic conditions, consistent with lower perceived risk in these groups. For COVID-19, the pattern reverses: quantitative ability, rather than choice consistency, is positively associated with vaccination as eligibility expands. Respondents with higher quantitative ability held more favorable beliefs about vaccine safety and effectiveness before most could obtain a dose, and these beliefs account for about 60 percent of the later widening in the ability-receipt association, suggestive evidence that the beliefs translated into vaccination. These results suggest that policies to raise uptake should differ between familiar and new vaccines: prosocial and norm-based messaging for the former, and early outreach that provides clear safety information and lowers the cost of acting for the latter.
